# Child play-test — observation protocol

15 children, one 15–20 minute prototype session each, plus a short parent debrief.

This session answers a question no parent interview can: **does the product work in a child's
hands without an adult rescuing it?** The brief's engagement gate depends entirely on this —
"most play-test children complete a 15-minute session without repeated adult rescue."

---

## Before the child arrives

- [ ] Parental consent signed (see `01-screener.md`) — no consent, no session
- [ ] Prototype pad, unbranded, at final paper weight
- [ ] **Three** coloring media on the table: wax crayons, colored pencils, and felt-tip markers
- [ ] Timer, visible to you and not to the child
- [ ] This sheet, one copy per child
- [ ] A second identical sheet the child can keep and take home

> The three media matter. Marker bleed-through on 140–160 gsm uncoated is the single most
> likely production failure in this product, and the only way to find it before a print run is
> to watch a real 6-year-old press hard with a real marker.

## Setting up the child

Say, in the child's language, then stop talking:

> "This is something I'm making for children. It isn't finished yet. Can you try it and tell me
> what you think? You can do anything you want with it — and if you get bored, that's fine,
> just tell me and we'll stop."

> «هذا شيء أسويه للأطفال، وما خلص بعد. ممكن تجربه وتقول لي رأيك؟ سوِّ فيه اللي تبي — وإذا
> مللت، عادي، بس قل لي ونوقف.»

Then **hand it over and be quiet**. Sit slightly to the side, not across the table. Do not
point at pages. Do not explain the prompts. Do not praise anything until the session ends.

If the child asks you what to do, answer once with: *"whatever you like"* — and note it as an
**instruction request** on the sheet. If they ask a second time, note it as a **rescue**.

## Parent debrief — 5 minutes, after the child has finished

1. Was that what you expected to see?
2. Did anything surprise you about how they used it?
3. Would this hold them for 15 minutes at home, without you?
4. Having watched that — what would you pay for it?
5. What would you change?

> Q4 gives you a **post-observation** price, which is a different and more informative number
> than the interview's unanchored guess. The gap between the two is itself a finding: if
> watching their own child use it moves the number up, your product page's job is to
> manufacture that same experience with video.

## Reading the results across all 15

Aggregate these before the gate review:

| Metric | Gate | Where it goes |
|--------|------|---------------|
| % completing 15 min without repeated rescue | **Gate: most children** | `05-decision-gates.md` |
| Median minutes to first disengagement | Should exceed 15 | Session-length claim on the PDP |
| % who noticed a discovery prompt | Low → the learning layer is invisible; redesign it | Product spec |
| % who acted on a creative prompt | Low → you have built a coloring book, not a discovery pad | Product spec |
| Any marker bleed-through | **Any** = raise paper weight or re-spec, before proofing | Printer brief |
| First-page-picked distribution | Cover and hero-image decision | `02-product/` |
| Ages 4 and 9 vs ages 5–8 | Confirms or breaks the 5–8 age lock | Positioning |

### What failure looks like, and what to do

- **Children finish in 6 minutes and leave** → line art is too sparse. Add detail density; do
  not add pages.
- **Children need constant help** → prompt language is above reading age. Simplify, or move the
  prompt to a parent-facing position on the sheet.
- **Nobody notices the discovery prompts** → the learning layer is decorative. This is a
  positioning failure, not a design nit: "creative time that *teaches*" is the lead message,
  and if the teaching is invisible in use, the message is unsupported.
- **Bleed-through on any sheet** → fix the paper before the proof stage. Bleed-through
  generates returns and one-star reviews, and it is the cheapest problem here to prevent and
  the most expensive to discover after a print run.
- **Ages 8–9 call it babyish** → confirms the brief's too-broad-age risk. Narrow the claim
  rather than widening the art.
