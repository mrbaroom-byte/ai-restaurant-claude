# How the launch price gets chosen

## The rule

> **Contribution per qualified visitor is the primary measure.** A higher price is acceptable
> only when the extra unit margin more than offsets the conversion loss *and* the return/refund
> risk.

Not conversion rate. Not revenue. Not what parents said they would pay.

```
contribution per visitor  =  contribution per order  ×  (orders ÷ qualified visitors)
```

The winner is the cell with the highest value on the left. That is the entire decision.

## Why the obvious alternatives are wrong

**Conversion rate** always favours the lower price, which is why it feels intuitive and why it
is wrong. SAR 39 will out-convert SAR 49 almost every time and still lose, because at the seeded
assumptions SAR 49 carries 2.4× the contribution. A 20% conversion penalty on a 144% margin
gain is a trade you take.

**Stated willingness to pay** — the survey's E3–E5 questions — overstates at the low end and
understates at the premium end, reliably. Parents underestimate what they will pay for something
they have not held, and they answer price questions as if they were being asked to justify a
budget. Use it to set the *range you test*, never the price you charge.

**Revenue** ignores the SAR 7 shipping subsidy, the SAR 1.98 payment fee and the 4% returns
reserve, all of which scale with orders. A revenue-maximising price can lose money per unit.

## Running the price cell

1. Run the **message** test first (Cells A vs B). Price is held identical across it — otherwise
   you cannot tell which variable moved the result.
2. Take the winning message. **Only inside it**, randomise price: SAR 39 vs SAR 49, pad vs bundle.
3. Keep everything else identical — same creative, same audience, same landing page, same day
   parts. One variable at a time or the test tells you nothing.
4. Minimum 200 qualified clicks per price cell. Below that you are reading noise.
5. Record contribution per visitor per cell in the sprint workbook's *Paid Test* tab, section 3.

## Before you crown a winner

- [ ] Both cells cleared 200 qualified clicks
- [ ] The difference is bigger than the noise — report the interval, not just the point estimate
- [ ] The winning cell's contribution per visitor is positive after actual observed CAC
- [ ] Preorder *quality* checked, not just count: are the orders from the target cities, and did
      any convert on the bundle?
- [ ] The result holds in **both** Riyadh and Jeddah, or you note explicitly that it does not

## If the two prices tie

They often will at these sample sizes. Then take **the higher price**, for three reasons:

1. It carries more contribution per order, which is the constraint the whole model is short of.
2. It leaves room to discount later. Launching at 39 and raising to 49 is far harder than
   launching at 49 and running a founder offer at 39.
3. The brief's positioning is premium, and a premium price is itself a quality signal — the
   product page's job is to earn it with paper, prompts, format and proof.

## What the founder preorder price is actually for

SAR 39 as a founder price is a **deliberate loss-leader against a thin contribution.** At the
seeded assumptions it clears under SAR 6 per order — before any acquisition cost at all.

That is acceptable, and only for these purposes:

- Buying the first verified-photo reviews, which cannot be bought later at any price
- Proving the operational flow — pack, ship, deliver, support — before the public launch
- Generating real-product media and user artwork for the Share stage

**Cap the quantity, date-limit it, and budget it as marketing spend, not revenue.** The failure
mode is that the founder price quietly becomes the launch price because nobody ever decided to
end it. Write the end date on the page before it goes live.

## The one thing that would change all of this

Repeat purchase. Every number above is a first-order calculation on a single transaction, and a
single-transaction business shipping one low-priced physical item at a time is genuinely hard —
that is what the required-conversion column is telling you.

The brief's answer is the expandable content world: series, seasonal capsules, sticker stories,
activity kits, postcards, educator packs. A second purchase from an existing customer carries no
acquisition cost, which means it converts at close to full contribution.

So the number worth tracking from the first order onward is **how many buyers come back**. Nothing
in this model can be fixed by better copy; a 30% repeat rate fixes most of it. Instrument for it
now — collect the email consent, tag the cohort, and measure it — rather than discovering in
January that you never set it up.
